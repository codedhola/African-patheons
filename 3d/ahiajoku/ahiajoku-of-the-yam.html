Igbo alụsị of yams and the harvest

Ahiajoku of the Yam

A photoreal study of the Igbo yam deity on the evening of her festival: a matching Akwete wrapper, blouse and head tie, coral at her throat, ivory at her wrists and ankles, a new yam held out in both hands, and the full moon rising behind the barn.

What is sourced, what is imagined

Many alụsị are understood as unseen forces, and the sources here describe no image of Ahiajoku at all. This figure is an interpretation in Igbo dress, not a cult image. This list keeps the two apart.

From the sources

  • The full moon. Her festival falls on a full moon before the New Yam festival, and new yams may not be eaten until the rite is done. Wikipedia: Ahia Njoku
  • One cloth, two pieces. Akwete is woven in identical pairs: one a wrapper, the other a stole, a second wrapper or a blouse. Wikipedia: Akwete cloth
  • The cloth's size and structure. Most Akwete is 40 to 50 inches wide. Motifs are a supplementary weft floating on a warp-faced plain weave. The map here is one 1.15 m width of loom, built from a 4.2 mm grid of floats. Wikipedia, The Met
  • Three or four motifs. Weavers claim over a hundred, with usually three or four on a cloth. Here: ikaki the tortoise, the saw and draughts, all named by weavers, and a lozenge like the one on a white-ground wrapper in the Met. Wikipedia, The Met
  • Ichafu. A headcloth tied flat on the head, worn with wrapper and blouse. Wikipedia: Igbo regalia and headdresses
  • Nkalari and odu. Large coral at the neck and wrists and as earrings; ivory at the wrists (odu aka) and ankles (odu ukwu). Wikipedia: Igbo regalia and headdresses
  • The barn. Posts 5 to 10 cm thick and about a metre apart, lighter cross members, tubers tied on one by one, shoots from live poles for shade. FAO: Traditional post-harvest technology

This study's choices

  • Her face, age and build. No source describes how she looks; she is drawn as a strong woman of about forty.
  • A goddess. The sources disagree. Wikipedia calls Ahia Njoku a goddess and treats Njoku Ji, the guardian of the yam, in a separate entry. Oriire calls Ahiajoku the yam god, a son of Ibo whose flesh germinated into yam. This figure follows the gallery.
  • The colours. No source gives her colours. Cream, green, red and indigo carry over from her figure in the gallery.
  • The ivory. Ivory at the wrists and ankles belongs to the women of the Otu Odu society. It is given to her here as a mark of standing.
  • The gesture. Holding the yam out in both hands is this study's own.
  • The evening. A full moon rises at sunset, opposite the sun, so she stands with the last warm light on her and the moon behind. The moon is drawn far larger than it would look through this lens.
  • The basket, the field and the barn's place in the picture. Set dressing, built to the measurements above where there are any.

How she was made

The body starts from MakeHuman's free base mesh, shaped with its targets and rigged. The arms and legs were solved to target positions, and each finger was closed joint by joint until it came to rest on the yam.

The wrapper is one cloth wound round the hips, with its outer edge lying down the front. The blouse is cut from the body itself, loosened, and simulated while the arms come down to hold the yam, so its folds come from that movement.

The cloth's pattern is laid out on a weaver's grid, float by float. The ichafu is a surface lifted off the skull, with its pleats worked out around the knot. The coral is laid on the chest as three nested ovals, and the yams in the basket were dropped in under rigid-body physics.

Every stage is a Python script in 3d/ahiajoku/blender/, using the toolkit in 3d/toolkit/, and the finished scene is 3d/ahiajoku/ahiajoku.blend.
