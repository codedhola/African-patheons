What is sourced, what is imagined

Ọ̀ṣun is worshipped at a living shrine, and none of the sources used here describes how she looks. What she wears here comes from her priestesses and devotees at Òṣogbo, so this figure is an interpretation, not a cult image. This list keeps the two apart.

From the sources

  • White cloth, made up here as ìró and bùbá. Her priests and priestesses wear white in her honour at Òṣogbo. Zikoko, Wikipedia
  • The Àgògo, its rising crest, the ìkódẹ feather and the Àrẹ́mọ́rùn choker, as photographed at the 2014 festival. Adunni Olorisha Trust, Oriire
  • Cowries in the hair. One writer lists parrot feathers, cowries and brass hair sticks as its adornments. Pulse
  • The brass fan, at the size of the High Museum’s, carried along the river by her priestesses. High Museum of Art
  • The ààjà, a brass or bronze bell rung when praying to her. Zikoko, Adunni Olorisha Trust
  • Yellow, coral, gold and white; the number five; amber and orange. All from her Wikipedia entry. Wikipedia: Oshun
  • The grove. A meandering river in primary high forest, regarded as her abode. UNESCO

This study’s choices

  • Her face, age and build. None of the sources used here describes how she looks.
  • Dressing her as her priestesses dress. She is the òrìṣà and they are people. The sources describe them, not her, so this is a borrowing.
  • The fish on the fan. The museum’s fan has two birds. Fish come from her gallery figure, and the river.
  • The bell’s shape, the sash and the gold lines in the cloth. Their colours are hers; the forms are designed.
  • Tìróò. A Yorùbá cosmetic, but no source puts it on her.
  • The bead colours in the choker, and how many strands she wears.
  • The morning and the place. An invented bend of the river. The grove’s real sculptures are left out, because they are the works of named artists.
  • Where sources differ. Sources differ on two things. Yellow and gold lead in Cuba and Brazil, while at Òṣogbo her priests wear white. And UNESCO gives the festival twelve days in July and August, Wikipedia about two weeks in August, and the Adunni Olorisha Trust ten to fourteen days in August.

How she was made

The body starts from MakeHuman’s free base mesh, shaped younger and softer than Ahiajoku’s and rigged. Each arm was solved to its object, and each finger closed at all three joints at once, as a real finger does, until it came to rest on the handle.

The ìró is one cloth wound round the hips. The bùbá is cut from the body with its hollows bridged, then simulated as the arms come into place. While it settles, the waist is drawn in and lifted, so the gathers above and below the sash form on their own, and the tied sash then presses the pouched cloth flat under it.

The Àgògo is built braid by braid: cornrows projected onto the scalp from the hairline to the crest, and rings of thicker braid running round the crest itself. The fan’s relief is one height map, modelled as a single sheet of brass.

Every stage is a Python script in 3d/osun/blender/, using the toolkit in 3d/toolkit/, and the finished scene is 3d/osun/osun.blend.
