Overview

What these traditions share

Africa has no single “traditional religion”: each people below has its own names, gods and practices, and scholars argue over how far general statements hold. Still, certain patterns recur from the Nile to the Cape.

  • A creator who stands back

    Almost every people here names a supreme creator: Olódùmarè, Chukwu, Nyame, Mawu, Roog, Ngai, Mwari, uMvelinqangi. That creator is rarely given shrines or daily offerings. Myths explain the distance, as when Nyame withdrew from an old woman’s pestle, and everyday religion turns to closer powers.

  • Powers with portfolios

    Between people and the creator stand òrìṣà, vodún, abosom, trɔ̃wo, alusi, bisimbi or mhondoro, each with a domain. Some pairings recur across borders: thunder joined to justice (Ṣàngó, Xɛvioso, Hebieso, Amadioha), iron to war and technology (Ògún, Gu), the earth to morality (Ala, Asase Yaa).

  • The dead remain family

    Ancestors are elders who have moved on, not distant ghosts. They are fed at blackened stools (Akan), served at altars with brass heads (Edo), embodied by masquerades (Egúngún, mmanwụ, the Dogon dama) and consulted through mediums. Becoming a good ancestor usually takes proper rites, such as the Shona kurova guva.

  • Divination as a way of knowing

    Ifá, Fa and Afa read 256 signs from palm nuts or a chain; Serer Saltigues forecast the rains; izangoma throw bones; Dogon diviners read fox tracks; the Maasai laibon prophesies. Divination diagnoses illness and guides decisions, from a family’s to a kingdom’s.

  • Sacred landscapes

    Groves (Osun-Osogbo), mountains (Kĩrĩnyaga, Ol Doinyo Lengai, Jebel Barkal), hills (Matobo), lakes (Bosomtwe, Hora Arsadi), rivers (the Osun, the Nile) and particular trees (the mũgumo fig, the Oromo odaa, adbar trees) are dwelling places of spirit, and several are now World Heritage Sites.

  • Religion as a way of life

    Many of the indigenous names mean custom or tradition, not “religion”: Ìṣẹ̀ṣe, Omenala, Chivanhu. There is no single scripture and no central authority; teaching lives in oral literature such as the Ifá verses, and moral law is guarded by the earth and by the community.

  • Encounter, suppression, revival

    Christianity and Islam now dominate almost everywhere, often layered with older practice. Colonial writers shaped what outsiders “know”, from Griaule’s Dogon to missionaries’ choice of God-names. Recent years have brought public revival: Ìṣẹ̀ṣe Day in Nigeria, Vodun Days in Benin, mass Irreecha gatherings and the return of the Benin Bronzes.

  • Across the Atlantic

    Yoruba, Fon-Ewe, Kongo and Akan traditions were rebuilt in the Americas as Candomblé, Lucumí, Vodou, Palo, Kumina and Winti, often by pairing African deities with Catholic saints. These diaspora forms now send pilgrims back to Ilé-Ifẹ̀, Òṣogbo and Ouidah.

Spelling

Names appear first in a local form, with tone marks or special letters where they are standard (ẹ, ọ, ṣ, ɔ, ɛ, ĩ, ũ, ǀ), followed by common variants and diaspora spellings in Portuguese, Spanish or Haitian Creole.

Days and colours

Sacred days and colours are lineage-specific. Where a colour is a Cuban or Brazilian convention, the entry says so.

Tense

Living traditions are described in the present tense. Ancient Egypt, Kush and the Fang byeri cult, which no longer function as they once did, are described in the past.

West Africa

From the Senegal River to the Niger Delta. The most densely documented religious systems on the continent, and the ones the Atlantic slave trade carried furthest. Divination by 256 signs, gods of thunder, iron and rivers, and masquerades that make the ancestors visible recur from people to people.

Supreme being
Olódùmarè (also Olórun, “owner of the heavens”, and Olófin) is the source of àṣẹ, the power that makes things happen. Olódùmarè is too vast for the human mind and usually has no shrines of its own; people approach it through the òrìṣà. Because there is no central authority, understandings vary from town to town and lineage to lineage.

Key myths

In the best-known account, Olódùmarè sent Ọbàtálá down from the sky on a chain carrying a snail shell of earth and a five-toed hen. Ọbàtálá drank palm wine and fell asleep, so Odùduwà finished the task: he poured out the earth and the hen scratched it across the water. Dry land spread at Ilé-Ifẹ̀, which became the sacred first city, and Odùduwà became ancestor of the Yoruba kings. Ọbàtálá went on to mould human bodies, and Olódùmarè breathed life into them.

Ṣàngó is remembered as an early king of Ọ̀yọ́ who became the òrìṣà of thunder. In Òṣogbo, the river goddess Ọ̀ṣun promised the town’s founders prosperity if they honoured her, a pact the kings of Òṣogbo still renew.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

The Ọ̀ṣun-Òṣogbo festival runs for about two weeks every August and ends in a procession to the river shrine in the Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2005 and probably the last of the sacred groves that once stood outside every Yoruba town. Ifá divination (UNESCO intangible heritage, proclaimed 2005, inscribed 2008) is consulted before any important decision: a babaláwo casts palm nuts or a chain to reach one of 256 odù, each with chanted verses (ẹsẹ). The Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ masquerade honours “the mothers” and is also on UNESCO’s list.

Ancestors

Egúngún masquerades, fully covered in layered cloth, are the ancestors returning to visit their descendants at annual festivals. Names such as Babátúndé (“father has returned”) and Yétúndé (“mother has returned”) express the belief that ancestors are reborn into the family. Each person’s orí (head, destiny) is chosen before birth, kneeling before Olódùmarè.

Today and in the diaspora

Most Yoruba today are Christian or Muslim, yet Ìṣẹ̀ṣe is gaining public ground. Osun State first set aside 20 August as Ìṣẹ̀ṣe Day in 2013–14; Ogun and Oyo followed in 2023, and on 20 August 2026 Lagos, Ogun, Oyo and Osun all declared a work-free day. Practitioners still report discrimination, and Egúngún and Orò outings have led to clashes.

Diaspora: the òrìṣà are the base of Cuban Regla de Ocha or Lucumí (Santería), Brazilian Candomblé (Ketu or Nagô nation), Umbanda, Trinidad Orisha and the Nago spirits of Haitian Vodou. In Cuba Changó was paired with Saint Barbara, Ochún with Our Lady of Charity and Yemayá with the Virgin of Regla.

Where accounts differ

  • Who made the land: Ife traditions credit Odùduwà after Ọbàtálá’s lapse; other versions credit Ọbàtálá alone, or say Olódùmarè sent seventeen òrìṣà to finish the work.
  • What the òrìṣà are: some describe them as emanations of Olódùmarè, others as divine beings who lived on earth or as deified ancestors.
  • Days and colours: the ritual week has four days, but A. B. Ellis (1894) counted it as five, which is why Ṣàngó’s day is sometimes called the “fifth”. The colours most often quoted are Cuban and Brazilian conventions; Nigerian lineages use their own bead codes.

Supreme being
Chukwu (Chi-ukwu, “great spirit”), also Chineke or Chiokike (the creator) and Obasi in some dialects. Chukwu has no form, is linked with the sun and sky, and cannot be fully grasped by human minds. Key myths

In Umu-Eri tradition, Chukwu sent Eri down from the sky to the Anambra (Omambala) valley at Aguleri and fed his family with heavenly food, which stopped when Eri died. His son Nri appealed to Chukwu and was told to sacrifice a son and a daughter. After three Igbo weeks (twelve days), yam grew from the son’s grave and cocoyam from the daughter’s. In exchange for sharing the new crops, Nri’s line received the right to cleanse abominations and to prepare the annual yam medicine, the basis of the Eze Nri’s ritual authority.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

The New Yam festival (Iri Ji or Iwa Ji) honours Ala and Ahiajoku and is kept even by Christians. In the Owerri area, communities built Mbari houses: open-sided shrines of anthill clay filled with painted figures of Ala and her children, Amadioha and scenes of daily life, made as a sacrifice to Ala and then left to crumble back into the earth. The Ijele masquerade was inscribed by UNESCO in 2009. Aguleri and Nri are the main ancestral sanctuaries.

Ancestors

The ancestors (ndichie) remain part of the lineage and are honoured at family shrines; masquerades (mmanwụ) are understood as ancestral spirits visiting the living. Ala keeps the dead in her womb, and ancestors are expected to return in new births. A man’s Ikenga shrine received prayers and sacrifices in return for ancestral guidance.

Today and in the diaspora

Christian missions largely replaced Odinani in the 20th century, but it remains strong in rural communities and very visible in harvest festivals and masquerades. Many Igbo Catholics hold Odinala values as part of their identity. A younger generation is reclaiming it as philosophy and heritage. Diaspora: Igbo captives were numerous in Virginia, Jamaica and Barbados, and Haitian Vodou keeps an “Ibo” nation of spirits.

Where accounts differ

  • Eri’s origin: oral tradition has him descend from the sky; other accounts, including some local ones, describe a migration from elsewhere, even from Egypt or ancient Israel. Scholars treat these origin claims as contested.
  • Seniority: Aguleri and Nri dispute which line is senior among Eri’s children.
  • Ekwensu: traditionally a trickster and spirit of war, recast as the Devil in mission translations.

Key myths

Osanobua sent his sons down to a world covered by water and let each choose a gift: wealth, wisdom, magical power. The youngest, prompted by a bird, took a worthless-looking snail shell. When they reached the water he turned it over and sand poured out without end, forming dry land. The land became his and Osanobua made him its king, which is why the Oba of Benin is owner of the land; a snail shell of earth is still part of the Oba’s coronation. The eldest brother became Olokun, ruler of the waters, whose underwater palace mirrors the Oba’s.

Kingship then passed from the Ogiso (“rulers of the sky”) to the dynasty founded by Eweka, son of Prince Oranmiyan of Ife.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

The Igue festival (December) renews the Oba’s mystical powers and blesses the kingdom. Olokun is worshipped with songs, drums, rattles and gongs to call the god’s attention. The Oba’s palace in Benin City is the ritual centre of the kingdom.

Ancestors

When a parent dies, the children set up an ancestral altar in their memory. In the palace, each new Oba commissioned a cast-brass commemorative head of his predecessor for the royal ancestral altars, along with carved ivory tusks. Many of these were looted in the 1897 British raid and are the objects now called the “Benin Bronzes”.

Today and in the diaspora

Traditional religion is thriving in Benin City despite the spread of Pentecostal churches, and Olokun remains the most important deity. Restitution is reconnecting objects with their shrines: the Smithsonian transferred 29 works to Nigeria in October 2022, Germany returned others the same year, and a presidential declaration of March 2023 vested ownership of returned artefacts in the Oba. Diaspora: Olokun is honoured in Cuban Lucumí and some Brazilian houses.

Where accounts differ

  • The creation story varies: three or four sons, a hornbill or toucan as the helpful bird, and in one museum version the youngest sells land to his brothers. One German museum text even calls Olokun the supreme god.
  • Dynastic origins: Jacob Egharevba’s 1934 history made the first Ogiso a son of Ife’s Oduduwa; Edo tradition treats the Ogiso as indigenous. Benin and Ife each describe the other’s ruler as a “son”.
  • Ownership of returned bronzes, state museum or Oba, became contested after the 2023 declaration.

Key myths

Nana Buluku bore the twins Mawu and Lisa and divided the world between them. Mawu-Lisa made the world in four days: on the first, the world and humans; on the second, the earth was made fit to live on; on the third, people received intellect, language and senses; on the fourth, technology. The cosmic serpent Aido-Hwedo carried the creator in its mouth while the earth was shaped. Mawu-Lisa then shared the realms among their children: earth to Sakpata, thunder to Xɛvioso, the sea to Agbe. The youngest, Legba, was left without a realm and became the messenger, the one go-between who can reach the creator.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Benin recognised Vodún as a religion in 1996, and 10 January is a national holiday. Since 2024 the government has staged Vodun Days in Ouidah around that date: initiates emerge in procession from their convents, and Zangbeto night guardians and Egungun masquerades perform. Thousands attend, including visitors from the African diaspora. Fa divination assigns each person a life sign (kpoli) among 256.

Ancestors

Lineages honour their founders and stools as part of the vodún order. Dahomey’s royal ancestors were served at Abomey, and that royal cult crossed the Atlantic: Zomadonu, a Dahomean royal vodún, gives the Casa das Minas in São Luís, Brazil, its formal name.

Today and in the diaspora

Vodún is practised openly in Benin alongside Christianity, with Ouidah as its spiritual capital; its keepers stress that it is no longer treated as sorcery. Diaspora: Haitian Vodou takes its name from the Fon word and keeps Fon spirits in its Rada nation (Legba, Danbala and others, named for the old kingdom of Allada). Fon and Ewe traditions also shaped Candomblé Jeje and Tambor de Mina in Brazil, Arará in Cuba, and Louisiana Voodoo.

Where accounts differ

  • Mawu and Lisa appear as twin siblings, as a married couple, or as a single two-faced being; whether Nana Buluku stands above them varies by account.
  • The seven children are listed differently; one tradition makes Sakpata Mawu-Lisa’s grandson rather than son.
  • Much of the English-language picture still rests on Melville Herskovits’s 1930s fieldwork in Abomey.

Key myths

Long ago Nyame lived so close to the earth that an old woman pounding fufu kept striking him with her pestle. Annoyed, he withdrew on high. She told her children to stack mortars to reach him; one short, they pulled one from the bottom and the tower fell.

Around 1700 the priest Okomfo Anokye called the Golden Stool (Sika Dwa Kofi) down from the sky onto the lap of Osei Tutu, the first Asantehene. It holds the sunsum (spirit) of the Asante nation, living, dead and unborn, and no one may sit on it. In 1900 the British governor’s demand to sit on it set off the War of the Golden Stool, led by Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Adae (“resting place”) is the main ancestral festival. The Akan calendar runs in 42-day cycles, nine to a year, and in each the ancestors are honoured on a Sunday (Akwasidae) and a Wednesday (Awukudae). Work stops, drummers invoke the ancestral drummers, priests hold an akɔm dance, and sheep’s blood and special foods are offered to the stools. At Akwasidae the Asantehene visits the Bantama royal mausoleum; the great Adae Kese gathers all Asante chiefs in Kumasi to renew allegiance to the Golden Stool. Lake Bosomtwe is a major sacred site.

Ancestors

The nsamanfo live in the land of the dead and watch over their descendants. Royal ancestors are “fed” at blackened wooden stools kept in stool rooms. Every rite begins with libation: Nyame is named first, then Asase Yaa, then the ancestors; at funerals a libation asks Asase Yaa’s permission to dig the grave.

Today and in the diaspora

Most Akan have been Christian since the early 20th century, often blending older practice, but kings keep their sacred role and Adae remains a major public occasion. Diaspora: Akan captives, called Coromantee, shaped Jamaican Maroon culture; Ananse stories spread across the Caribbean and the American South; and Suriname’s Winti religion draws on Akan, Fon and Kongo roots.

Where accounts differ

  • A remote god? The “withdrawn” Nyame of early missionary accounts sits uneasily with reports of active household devotion to him.
  • The pestle story was told to 19th-century missionaries asking about the creator; the scholar Jan Platvoet argues it is a moral tale about old women’s place in society rather than a sacred myth.
  • Asase Yaa is Nyame’s wife in some accounts and his daughter in others; her day is Thursday for most Akan but Friday for the Fante.

Key myths

The Ewe say they once lived in the walled city of Notsie (in present-day Togo) under King Agɔkɔli, whose cruelty drove them to flee. In many tellings the women softened a section of wall by throwing water on it daily until it could be pushed through. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Hogbetsotso (“festival of the exodus”) is held at Anloga, the Anlo ritual capital, with its grand durbar on the first Saturday of November. It is preceded by a state divination by seers from across the Anlo towns, rites to drive out disease (dodede), reconciliation (nugbidodo) and the purification of black stools with libation. Dancers perform Agbadza and the backward-stepping Husago that re-enacts the escape. Yeve initiations and Afa celebrations continue in many towns.

Ancestors

Ancestral spirits belong to the clan as a whole. Lineage stools are central to Ewe and Fon families and are purified at festivals. The Ewe also speak of si, a spiritual marriage that binds a devotee to a deity.

Today and in the diaspora

Christianity arrived with traders and missionaries and is now widespread, but shrine towns such as Klikor keep traditional religion active, and Hogbetsotso draws national leaders. Diaspora: Ewe and Fon (“Jeje”) traditions shaped Candomblé Jeje and Tambor de Mina in Brazil, where songs are still sung in Jeje, and Haitian Vodou.

Where accounts differ

  • Who Mawu is: for most Ewe the androgynous supreme being; for the neighbouring Fon, the female half of Mawu-Lisa. Some Ewe describe Mawu like the Christian God, others as a diffuse life force.
  • Festival date: most sources say the first Saturday of November; some travel guides give Sunday.
  • Before Notsie: stories of an older homeland further east are tradition, not established history.

Key myths

Amma created the universe; the first beings were the Nommo, who multiplied into four pairs of twins. One Nommo was sacrificed and its body scattered to purify the world, which is why Binu shrines are spread across the Dogon country. The ancestors came down to earth in an ark, bringing weaving, smithing and farming. Lebe, the oldest ancestor, seemed to die but was restored as a serpent; a fragment of his first altar was used to found the Lebe altar of every new village. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

The Sigi (Sigui) is held once every sixty years to mark the passing of one generation to the next. It begins at Youga Dogorou and moves from village to village over about seven years; young men learn a secret language (sigi so) and carve a new Great Mask, and the old one is laid in a burial cave. The last Sigi ran from 1967 into the early 1970s and was filmed by Jean Rouch. Dama, performed by the Awa mask society with masks such as the kanaga, ends mourning and sends the dead to the ancestors. Bulo (sowing) and Goru (harvest) bring the Hogon and the Binu priests together. The Cliff of Bandiagara (289 villages) has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1989.

Ancestors

Ancestor veneration is the core of Dogon practice: the mask society, the Lebe cult, the Binu shrines and the lineage altars (wagem) in each patriarch’s house. After the dama, the soul departs to Amma. The Hogon is held to be the oldest direct descendant of the first ancestor.

Today and in the diaspora

Fewer than half the Dogon are Muslim and a smaller share Christian, often combining faiths. Since about 2016, conflict between Dogon and Fulani communities in central Mali has damaged villages and stopped tourism; UNESCO and the ALIPH foundation fund reconstruction of the built heritage. A Dogon journalist is preparing to film the coming Sigi. There is no Dogon religion in the Atlantic diaspora, but Dogon cosmology entered popular culture through the “Sirius mystery” books of the 1970s.

Britannica still repeats the claim; Guinness World Records ties the sixty-year cycle to a human lifetime instead.
  • Next Sigi: the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts says it begins in 2027; Guinness says 2032; a Polish museum says 2026.

Kongo tradition says the great god Ne Kongo brought the first sacred medicine (nkisi) down from heaven in a clay pot set on three stones. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

A sculptor and a ritual expert (nganga) together made a nkisi; the nganga’s medicines turned the carving into an object that could heal illness, settle disputes and punish wrongdoers. With the nkisi nkondi, parties to an oath or lawsuit drove in a nail or blade to seal it. Ritual experts trained to commune with the ancestors and honoured them with shrines, sacred trees and minkisi in the forest (mfinda).

Ancestors

Ancestors are the centre of Kongo religion. They keep watch over their descendants, and some are believed to dwell in the forest or to return as bisimbi nature spirits. Nzambi, Mpungu, the bakulu and the minkisi together make up the spiritual world.

Today and in the diaspora

The Kingdom of Kongo adopted Catholicism from the late 1400s, so Kongo Christianity and ancestral practice have long interwoven; clan histories are still being published and taught. Diaspora: Kongo religion is the root of Cuban Palo (Regla de Congo), whose nganga cauldron descends from the minkisi; of Jamaican Kumina, brought by Central African indentured workers after emancipation; of the Kongo and Petwo spirits of Haitian Vodou; and of Brazil’s Candomblé Angola nation, where Nzambi is the “sovereign master”.

  • Only some sources give Nzambi a female counterpart, and Christian influence since the 15th century makes the “pre-Christian” layer hard to separate.
  • Museum texts differ on whether nkisi figures represent the spirit or only house it.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

In the byeri cult, skulls, femurs and vertebrae of revered ancestors (founders, leaders and fertile women) were kept in bark boxes in shrine houses, each topped by a carved guardian figure. Ancestors

Ancestor veneration is the centre of Fang religion. The ancestors set the moral standard of the community and speak to their descendants in dreams and visions. Fang society is patrilineal, so male ancestors are more often revered.

Today and in the diaspora

Colonial-era conversion to Christianity was massive, and the old byeri cult largely ended; many guardian figures are now in museums such as the Brooklyn Museum and the Met. Bwiti remains a living religion in Gabon. There is no Fang religion in the Atlantic diaspora.

  • Creator names are inconsistent: Mebere as the creator with Nzame as an aspect, Mebeghe as creator and Nzame as teacher, or Nzame as a trinity.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Ancestors

Orthodox practice honours the dead with prayers and memorial meals on fixed days after death rather than with an ancestor cult. Zar spirits, however, are often passed down within families.

Today and in the diaspora

Most Amhara are Orthodox Christians, so much so that “Amhara” was once used to mean Abyssinian Christian. Zar persists in Gondar and among Ethiopian communities abroad, including in Israel. Diaspora: Ethiopian Orthodox churches worldwide; the zar cult itself spread to Sudan, Egypt, Arabia and Iran, probably carried by the 19th-century slave trade.

  • Aksumite gods: sources give Astar as sky god or as sun and moon, and Beher as sea or land.

Today and in the diaspora

Enkai once lived among the people and their cattle. When sky and earth separated, Enkai and the cattle were above and the Maasai below, so Enkai sent the cattle down to them. In a widely told version they were lowered on a rope or leather strap from the sky. Since all cattle were given to the Maasai, taking cattle from others was once seen as recovering what was rightfully theirs, a belief now much less common.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Three linked male rites of passage are on UNESCO’s Urgent Safeguarding List (2018): Enkipaata, the induction of boys before initiation; Eunoto, the shaving of the young warriors (moran) on their way to adulthood; and Olng’esherr, the meat-eating ceremony that ends warriorhood and begins eldership. The central religious figure is the laibon (oloiboni), a hereditary ritual expert who heals, divines and prophesies and ensures success in war or rain. Prayers are offered at the foot of Ol Doinyo Lengai.

Ancestors

There is little emphasis on an ancestor cult or on the afterlife. Older outside accounts, such as J. H. Patterson’s in 1907, report that the dead were left in the bush rather than buried, with burial reserved for great leaders. Burial is common today, especially among Christians.

Today and in the diaspora

Many Maasai are Christian, and some Muslim. The rites of passage still draw large crowds but are declining as herding gives way to farming, land tenure changes and climate change hits cattle. A UNESCO-supported project (2017–2020) trained members of all nine clans to document and pass them on.

Where accounts differ

  • One god or two: Narok and Nanyokie are usually two moods of one god, but some myths treat them as brothers.
  • Death and burial are described mostly by outsiders, often colonial ones, and practice has changed a great deal.

uNkulunkulu came out of a bed of reeds (uhlanga) and brought forth people and cattle. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Izangoma (diviners) are called by the ancestors, often through illness and dreams; they consult the ancestors by throwing bones, while izinyanga work mainly with herbal medicines. Birth, puberty, marriage and death are each marked by slaughtering animals for the ancestors, with food, snuff and traditional beer. The annual Umkhosi woMhlanga (Reed Dance) at the royal Enyokeni palace is a major public celebration of young women.

Ancestors

The amadlozi (from dloza, “to care for, keep an eye on”) are respected as family elders, not worshipped as gods. Senior ancestors, the amathongo, guard the umsamo, the sacred back part of the home. When misfortune strikes, the ancestors are said to have “turned their backs”. Not everyone who dies becomes a benevolent ancestor; proper rites must bring the spirit home.

Today and in the diaspora

Many Zulu became Christian under colonialism, and many now combine church membership with ancestral rites, especially in cities; independent churches blend the two. Diviners and herbalists remain central to health and wellbeing. There is no Atlantic diaspora religion.

The calling of a diviner is told as a journey under the river. Amid a lung-sickness epidemic and decades of frontier war, chiefs including Sarhili backed the prophecy. When the appointed day in February 1857 passed, famine followed.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Ulwaluko, male initiation, takes boys through circumcision by a traditional surgeon (ingcibi), seclusion in the bush, and return as a man (indoda), reintroduced to the ancestors and uQamata; intonjane is the female initiation. Ancestors

The izinyanya guide and protect their descendants and speak in dreams, which are central to a diviner’s skill. Ritual slaughter at life’s turning points keeps the relationship with them in good order.

Today and in the diaspora

Christianity spread widely only after war, drought and disease had shaken Xhosa society; today many Xhosa combine church and ancestral practice, and independent churches blend the two. Ulwaluko remains widely practised and highly respected, but deaths in initiation schools (21 boys in one Eastern Cape summer season in 2019) have brought government health intervention.

Where accounts differ

  • Blame and meaning: colonial records blamed the chiefs or the prophetess; historians read it as a millenarian movement shaped by colonial pressure and cattle disease. The colony then used the famine to take land and push survivors into labour on settler farms.

Oral traditions disagree about how the Mwari cult reached the Matobo Hills. One says a group of priests broke away from Great Zimbabwe and founded it there. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

Rain-making pilgrims visit Njelele in August and September, guided by its custodians; at home, communities are cleansed of anything thought to drive rain clouds away, seed is blessed and first fruits are offered. A person who dies becomes a mudzimu only through rites: kurova gata soon after death and kurova guva at least a year later. All-night bira ceremonies with mbira music call the ancestors. Ancestors

The vadzimu protect their descendants from evil and bring peace and prosperity; misfortune sends people to a svikiro (medium) to learn the cause and make amends. Today and in the diaspora

Many Shona are Christian, often keeping ancestral rites as well. Njelele is still consulted, including by liberation-war veterans and politicians, though disputes over its custody have led to damage, including an attack with explosives. Practitioners reject the term “ancestor worship”, since the religion has no worship or prayer to ancestors in that sense.

North Africa and the Nile Valley

The longest written record of African religion runs from the Pyramid Texts to the last temple at Philae. Upriver, the kings of Kush built their own theology around Amun and the lion god Apedemak. Across the Maghreb, older Amazigh rites survive inside Islamic practice.

Key myths

The central myth is that of Osiris, a divine king murdered by his brother Set. His sister and wife Isis gathered his body and revived him long enough to conceive Horus; Osiris became ruler of the dead, and Horus won the throne after a long struggle with Set. The story tied the kingship of each pharaoh, the living Horus, to cosmic order. In the hall of judgement, a dead person’s heart was weighed against the feather of Maat.

Festivals, rites, sacred sites

At the Opet festival in Thebes, held during the Nile flood, the sacred boats of Amun, Mut and Khonsu were carried from Karnak to Luxor temple, renewing the king’s divine power. In the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, Amun crossed to the west bank, home of the tombs and royal memorial temples. Daily temple rites were performed by priests on behalf of the king. Karnak, Luxor, Abydos, Memphis, Heliopolis, Dendera and Philae were among the great sacred sites.

Ancestors

The dead were sustained with offerings at their tombs, and mummification preserved the body for the afterlife. During the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, Theban families visited their ancestors’ tombs with flowers, food and drink, feasting with the dead; the king used the same festival to seek his royal ancestors’ blessing.

Today and in the diaspora

Christianity and later Islam replaced the old cults; the last temple, the Isis temple at Philae, was closed in the 6th century. The Egyptian language survives in the liturgy of the Coptic Church. Small modern Kemetic movements, mostly outside Egypt, seek to revive the religion, and Egyptian gods remain among the best-known deities worldwide.

Where accounts differ

  • Rival theologies: each great city made its own god the creator; Memphis priests even claimed that Ptah brought Atum into being. Ra, Amun and Isis each rose and fell in importance over 3,000 years.
  • Many gods or one? Some Egyptologists describe the official religion as polytheism shaped by a near-monotheistic piety among the devout.

Kushite kings presented themselves as sons of Amun, chosen at Jebel Barkal. As the 25th Dynasty they ruled Egypt itself (about 747–656 BCE), reviving temple-building and pyramid burial. Hymns in the Lion Temple at Musawwarat es-Sufra praise Apedemak as a creator and giver of fertility as well as a warrior. Festivals, rites, sacred sites

After Egyptian rule ended, Kushites expanded Amun’s temples and adopted Egyptian funerary belief, including Osiris and the underworld. In the Meroitic period they began pilgrimages to holy sites, leaving carved images and texts on temple walls. In modern Nubian villages, on the seventh day after a birth, mother and child go to the Nile, offer food to the river angels and bring river water home, where it stays for the forty days of confinement. Jebel Barkal (2003) and the Island of Meroë with Musawwarat and Naqa (2011) are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Ancestors

Kushites communed with dead kings and ancestors by pouring wine or beer onto stone offering tables set outside their tombs, and kings were buried under pyramids at el-Kurru, Nuri and Meroë. Modern Nubian funerals blend Islamic and much older practice, with feasts and periodic sacrifices for the dead.

Today and in the diaspora

The Aswan High Dam flooded Old Nubia, and its people were moved in 1963–64; Nile rituals continue in changed form, though the dam and pollution have altered the river they honour. Since April 2023, Sudan’s war has devastated heritage: the Sudan National Museum in Khartoum was looted of most of its collection, UNESCO has funded emergency protection at Meroë and Jebel Barkal, and by January 2026 Sudan had recovered 570 objects, roughly 30% of what was lost. Many Nubians now live in Cairo, Alexandria and other cities.

  • Amun and Apedemak: whether their roles were complementary or overlapping is still unresolved, and scholars question whether the Egyptological idea of a “state god” fits Kush at all.
  • Continuity: how far modern Nubian river rites preserve ancient religion, rather than local Islam, is debated by anthropologists.

Anzar, lord of the rain, fell in love with a beautiful young woman. Each time he came near she fled in fear, and Anzar withdrew, holding back his rain until the land withered. In a drought, villagers carried a ladle dressed as a bride, Taghonja (Talghenja), in procession, singing for rain. The Greek historian Herodotus wrote that the ancient Nasamones swore oaths and sought dreams at the tombs of their most respected ancestors. In Islamic times, veneration of holy men’s tombs and their baraka (blessing) carries that respect for the sacred dead forward.

Today and in the diaspora
